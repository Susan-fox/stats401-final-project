US Flight Delays: From Symptoms to Source

Travelers blame the airport, the airline, or the weather. The Bureau of Transportation Statistics records a different leading cause — and it points inward, at the network's own schedule.

Dataset Data acquisition & analysis

Raw data

Twelve monthly files from the BTS Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance database, covering January–December 2024. Each file is one row per scheduled domestic flight operated by a carrier accounting for at least 0.5% of domestic passenger revenue.

  • Source: BTS TranStats, database EFD ( field definitions )
  • Acquisition: scripted download of the pre-packaged monthly archives from the /PREZIP/ endpoint rather than the interactive form
  • Volume: ≈7 million rows × 110 columns, about 2.5 GB uncompressed
  • Secondary: OpenFlights airport database , trimmed to US airports with IATA codes, for the latitude and longitude that BTS does not supply

The raw files are not committed. data/raw/ is in .gitignore ; scripts/build_data.py regenerates everything downstream of it.

Processing completed

We read only the 27 columns our questions need, using DuckDB to query the CSVs directly rather than loading them into memory. Four issues in this dataset needed decisions rather than defaults:

  1. The five cause columns are conditionally populated. They carry values only when arrival delay reaches 15 minutes. Blank is not zero, so every cause share is computed over delayed flights, never over all flights.
  2. Cancelled and diverted flights have null arrival delay. Dropping them silently would reward airports that cancel aggressively, so they are excluded from delay statistics and reported separately as a cancellation rate ( — of scheduled flights).
  3. Times are hhmm integers, not timestamps. 1435 means 14:35, and 2400 is a legal value meaning midnight. Departure hour is derived with integer division and a modulo, and durations come from ActualElapsedTime rather than subtracting arrival from departure, because overnight flights arrive at a smaller clock value than they departed.
  4. Two-letter carrier codes have been reassigned between airlines historically. We key on DOT_ID_Reporting_Airline and treat the letter code as a display label only.

Interaction and Animation meaningful exploration

Our interaction design is intended to connect the visualizations rather than treat each view as an independent chart. The final system will allow users to move from a national pattern to a selected airport, time period, carrier, or route-length range while preserving a consistent visual story.

Visualization 1 — map

Clicking an airport will select it and broadcast the selection to other views. Shift-click will allow comparison between two airports. A month slider will update the circles over time, using animated transitions so seasonal changes can be followed without re-reading a completely new map. A measure toggle will switch between median delay, 15-minute delay rate, and cancellation rate. Route arcs associated with a selected airport will appear only after selection so they do not compete visually with the airport symbols by default.

Visualization 2 — heatmap

The heatmap will filter to the airport selected in Visualization 1. Hovering a cell will provide exact values for a particular weekday and hour. Users will also be able to brush a range of departure hours to focus subsequent analysis on a particular part of the day. A month control will support exploration of seasonal differences.

Visualization 3 — cause composition

Clicking a delay-cause segment will re-sort carriers by the selected cause share. The rows will transition smoothly to make the new ranking readable rather than jumping instantly. Hovering one cause will visually emphasize that cause across all carriers. We also plan to support switching between carrier-level and airport-level cause composition.

Visualization 4 — route length

Hovering each distance group provides exact values for flight count, median delay, interquartile range, 90th-percentile delay, delay rate, and late-aircraft share. In the final version, users will be able to select or brush distance ranges so that route-length categories can be compared more directly with patterns in the other visualizations. This interaction supports the question of whether distance changes typical delay or mainly affects the extreme tail of the distribution.

Planned Visualization 5 — aircraft delay chain

The final visualization will follow a single aircraft across multiple flights during one operating day. Scheduled and actual timing for each leg will be placed on a common time axis so users can see whether an early delay disappears, remains stable, or grows across subsequent legs. Users will be able to inspect individual legs and compare scheduled versus actual turnaround time. Aircraft examples will be selected using a reproducible rule, such as tail numbers with many legs on a high-delay day, rather than being manually cherry-picked.

Evaluation Plan user testing

What we will evaluate

  • Comprehension. Can a first-time viewer read the correct value and identify the intended trend from each visualization without our narration?
  • Argument transfer. After using the site, can a viewer explain where flight delay primarily comes from and how delay can accumulate through the system?
  • Interaction discoverability. Can viewers find selections, filtering, and coordinated interactions without being explicitly told where every control is?
  • Performance. How quickly does the page render, and do interactions remain smooth on both laptop and mobile screens?

How

We plan to conduct task-based testing with 6–8 participants drawn from classmates and other students without specialized aviation knowledge. Each individual session will last approximately 15 minutes and use a think-aloud protocol. Example tasks include: identify an airport with unusually high typical delay ; decide whether an early-morning or evening departure appears safer and explain why ; and identify a carrier with a particularly high share of inherited late-aircraft delay . We will record task completion, time, errors, and explanations.

We will also conduct a heuristic review based on visualization principles from the course, including expressiveness, effectiveness, appropriate use of visual channels, colour discriminability, and whether the interface encourages comparisons that are actually supported by the data.

What we will collect

  • Task completion and error rate for each task
  • Time on task
  • Think-aloud comments and points of confusion
  • Self-rated confidence from 1–5 for each task
  • A short written explanation of the main source and pattern of delay before and after using the visualization
  • Browser performance timing during loading and transitions

Evaluation findings will be documented in docs/evaluation.md together with the design changes that resulted from each finding. This will allow the final report to describe not only that evaluation occurred, but also how it influenced the final design.

Next Steps

Our next implementation step is the single-aircraft daily flight-chain visualization . This view will connect the aggregate evidence in the first four visualizations to an aircraft-level example, allowing users to examine how a delay can persist, increase, or recover across sequential legs. After all five views are implemented, we will focus on coordinated interactions, final annotation and storytelling, evaluation, documentation, and presentation preparation.
