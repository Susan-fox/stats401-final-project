US Flight Delays: From Symptoms to Source

Flight delays are easy to notice but harder to explain. This project uses more than seven million scheduled U.S. domestic flights from 2024 to move from visible delay patterns toward their possible operational sources.

Where the delay minutes actually come from

Every minute of delay on a flight arriving at least 15 minutes late is attributed by the carrier to one of five reported causes. When the dominant category is late inbound aircraft, the delay did not begin on the current flight: it was inherited from an earlier leg in the aircraft's schedule.

Questions

The visualization story follows five related questions. The first three describe what delay looks like from the outside; the last two test whether it behaves like something the network carries forward through its own schedule.

  1. Which airports and regions experience the highest levels of delay?
  2. How do delay patterns vary by time of day and weekday?
  3. What share of reported delay is associated with late aircraft, carrier operations, the national airspace system, weather, and security?
  4. Does a delay early in an aircraft's day persist across its later legs, and does the shape of the rotation — short-haul or long-haul — change how far it travels?
  5. Which airports absorb the delay that arrives with an aircraft, and which hand more of it back to the network?

Dataset acquisition and processing

Primary data

The primary dataset is the 2024 Reporting Carrier On-Time Performance database published by the U.S. Bureau of Transportation Statistics. The data are distributed as monthly files, with one row for each scheduled domestic flight reported by qualifying carriers.

  • Source: BTS TranStats
  • Field definitions: BTS variable documentation
  • Acquisition: the twelve 2024 monthly archives were downloaded from the BTS pre-packaged download endpoint and processed programmatically
  • Volume: — scheduled flights

Airport coordinates

BTS does not provide latitude and longitude in the flight records, so airport coordinates were joined from the OpenFlights airport database using IATA airport codes.

  • Secondary source: OpenFlights airport database

Processing decisions

The raw files are too large to load directly in a browser, so Python and DuckDB are used to select, clean, aggregate, and export only the information needed by the visualizations.

  1. Delay causes are conditionally reported. CarrierDelay, WeatherDelay, NASDelay, SecurityDelay, and LateAircraftDelay are populated for sufficiently delayed flights. Missing cause values are therefore not treated as ordinary zeroes when calculating cause composition.
  2. The cause categories are attributions, not physical causes. BTS documents that the weather field covers only extreme weather; ordinary weather is folded into the national airspace category alongside traffic volume and air traffic control, and no cause is recorded for the delay inside the late-aircraft category. The weather share reported here is therefore a lower bound, and the argument below rests on the size of the late-aircraft category rather than on weather being small.
  3. Cancelled and diverted flights require separate treatment. Flights without a normal arrival-delay value are excluded from ordinary arrival-delay summaries and tracked separately through cancellation and diversion outcomes. The annual cancellation rate is — .
  4. Scheduled times are encoded as hhmm values. Values such as 1435 represent 14:35, and 2400 is a legal value, so departure hours are decoded with integer arithmetic rather than string slicing. Flight durations are taken from the reported elapsed time rather than by subtracting departure from arrival, because overnight flights land at a smaller clock value than they left.
  5. Aircraft rotations are reconstructed from tail numbers. Flights are grouped by Tail_Number and date and ordered by scheduled departure. Airline operations run on UTC while BTS publishes local clock times, so each rotation is checked for continuity — the destination of one leg must be the origin of the next — and any aircraft-day that fails is discarded rather than drawn in the wrong order. The share discarded is reported in sequence_diag.json and discussed as a limitation.

How to Explore

The five views are designed as one connected story rather than as independent charts. Hover over marks for exact values, use the controls above individual charts to change the comparison, and select an airport to connect the geographic, temporal, and turnaround views.

Reading the numbers. Every delay figure is actual time minus scheduled time, in minutes, so a positive value means late and a negative value means early. Negative medians are normal rather than surprising: schedules carry enough padding that the typical flight lands ahead of its scheduled arrival, which is also why Visualization 5 restricts its headline measure to flights that genuinely arrived late. The one figure that reverses this is recovered minutes, which is written so that positive means delay was absorbed on the ground.

  • Map: switch the state shading between delay rate and cancellation rate, or click an airport to filter the heatmap and highlight that airport in Visualization 5. Click it again to clear the selection.
  • Cause chart: click a cause button to re-sort carriers by that cause. Hovering a cause highlights the same category across carriers.
  • Propagation chart: switch between short-haul and long-haul rotations, change the measure, and click a legend entry to isolate one group and reveal the spread around its median.
  • Turnaround chart: drag a box to summarise a group of airports, switch the vertical axis between departure delay and delay kept, filter to one carrier, or click an airport to send it to the map and heatmap.

Implemented Interactions coordinated exploration

Interaction is used for filtering, changing variables, highlighting, brushing, coordinated selection, and details on demand. The project does not rely on tooltips alone.

1. Geographic metric switching

Users can switch the state map between 15-minute delay rate and cancellation rate. The state colors and the legend, including its numeric precision, transition to the newly selected measure while airport volumes remain fixed — so the reader can see which states look calm on one measure and severe on the other.

2. Coordinated airport selection

Selecting an airport is a single shared state rather than a per-chart control. Clicking an airport on the map filters the heatmap and highlights the same airport in the turnaround plot; clicking a point in the turnaround plot does the same in reverse. Clicking the selected airport again clears it everywhere.

3. Heatmap reset and details

The heatmap displays the active geographic selection and includes a reset control. Hovering individual cells reveals exact median delay, delay rate, flight count, weekday, and scheduled departure hour.

4. Cause-based carrier sorting and highlighting

Clicking one of the five reported delay causes reorders the carrier rows by that category's share, with animated transitions preserving visual continuity during the change in ranking. Hovering one category emphasizes it across every carrier and fades the others, which is the comparison a reader wants when all bars are the same width.

5. Rotation filtering and measure switching

The propagation chart can be switched between a flow view and median curves, filtered to short-haul or long-haul rotations, and — in the median view — switched between median delay, the gap against the on-time group, the 90th percentile, and the share of flights delayed 15 minutes or more. The flow view is read one cohort at a time: a control selects which starting group to follow, and clicking any band inside the diagram narrows it further, fading everything that did not come from that band. Both exist because the unconditioned diagram is dominated by the on-time majority and says very little about the question. Changing the measure tests whether a pattern is a property of the typical flight or only of the extreme tail — and, in the case of the gap measure, separates delay that is still being carried from the regression to the mean that any group defined by an extreme first leg would show anyway.

6. Series isolation with uncertainty on demand

Clicking a legend entry in the propagation chart isolates that group and reveals its interquartile band. The bands are hidden by default because four overlapping ranges obscure the comparison the chart exists to support; showing one at a time keeps the spread available without that cost.

7. Brushing with a selection summary

Dragging a box across the turnaround plot dims the airports outside it and reports what the selected group has in common: how many of them add delay, their median scheduled ground time, their median delay kept, and the number of turnarounds behind those figures. Brushing therefore answers a question rather than only dimming marks.

8. Axis and carrier switching in the turnaround view

The turnaround chart switches its rows between scheduled ground-time bands and individual airports, keeping the same marks so the two read alike, and the airport view can be re-ranked by most recovered, least recovered, or busiest. Changing the unit is the point rather than a convenience. At the airport level the medians differ by only a few minutes and the signal is buried in the variation between an airport's own turnarounds; grouping the turnarounds themselves by the slack the schedule allowed puts millions of observations behind every row. The airport view can also be filtered to a single carrier, which asks whether an airport behaves the same way for everyone operating there.

9. Details on demand

Hover tooltips throughout the project provide exact values without permanently adding labels that would clutter the visualizations. In the propagation chart the tooltip follows a vertical guide and reports all four groups at once, because the comparison of interest is across groups at the same leg.

Design Rationale

Each visualization uses an idiom matched to a different analytical task. Geographic position supports regional and airport comparison; a matrix supports repeated temporal comparison; normalized stacked bars support part-to-whole comparison; a flow diagram shows movement between states over a sequence, which is the shape of the propagation question itself; and a scatter plot with a fitted line supports relationship discovery.

Two encoding decisions are load-bearing. In the map, the state measure and the airport volume are given separate channels, because one circle carrying both made the dense corridors unreadable. In the turnaround chart, arrival and departure delay share one horizontal scale so the distance between the two dots is directly the minutes given back, with no second axis to reconcile.

Color is deliberately restrained. The map and heatmap use a single-hue sequential ramp whose domain is taken from the data rather than anchored at zero: median arrival delay is negative at almost every airport, so a ramp anchored at zero would leave half of its range unused and show the reader colors that never appear on the chart. Delay causes use a small categorical palette in which related colors separate the airline's own operation from external conditions. The propagation chart orders its four colors by severity, with the on-time group deliberately quiet, because it is the baseline the other three are read against.

Because flight-delay distributions are highly skewed, several views emphasize medians, quartiles, and upper percentiles. This reduces the risk that a small number of extreme events dominate the interpretation of the typical flight.

Evaluation

The project can be evaluated through task-based testing with users who have general familiarity with charts but no specialized aviation knowledge. Evaluation should focus on whether viewers can understand the visual encodings, discover the interactions, and correctly describe the relationship among the five views.

Representative tasks include:

  • identify a state or airport with relatively high delay
  • compare morning and evening delay patterns
  • identify which carriers have relatively high shares of a selected delay cause
  • state whether an aircraft that starts its day an hour late is still late by its fourth leg
  • say roughly how much of a 40-minute late arrival is given back when the schedule allows 40 minutes on the ground, and when it allows two hours

Beyond task accuracy, the measure that matters most is whether the project moves a belief: participants are asked in one sentence where flight delay mostly comes from, both before and after using the site. Other useful measures include completion time, interaction errors, self-reported confidence, and qualitative comments about confusing encodings or controls.

From Symptoms to Source

The five views form a progressively narrower investigation of U.S. flight delays. The map begins with the geographic distribution of delay. The heatmap shows that performance degrades across the operating day rather than varying at random. The cause chart shifts attention from visible outcomes to the categories carriers use to explain them, where the largest category points back at an earlier flight. The propagation chart tests that claim directly, following every usable aircraft-day across its successive legs. Finally, the turnaround chart asks what decides whether a late arrival is handed onward, and finds the answer in the one quantity a timetable actually sets: how long the aircraft is given on the ground.

Measuring that last question correctly required conditioning on the aircraft actually arriving late. Across all turnarounds, nearly every airport appears to depart later than it arrived — but only because schedules are padded on the inbound side, so the median aircraft lands early and pushes back close to on time. The apparent effect is a property of the timetable rather than of the airport.

Together, these views show why a delayed flight should not always be interpreted as an isolated event. In a tightly connected airline network, the outcome observed on one flight may reflect conditions and disruptions carried forward from earlier operations. The interactive structure lets users move between system-level patterns and specific airports rather than relying on a single summary statistic.

Two limitations deserve statement rather than a footnote. The first is that grouping rotations by their first leg guarantees some regression to the mean, so the decline along each line in Visualization 4 overstates how much the system recovers. The gap measure is included for exactly that reason: it reports the distance between groups, which regression cannot manufacture. The second is the reconstruction of aircraft rotations. BTS publishes local clock times while airline operations run on UTC, so rotations are ordered by local scheduled departure and then checked for continuity, and any aircraft-day whose legs do not form an unbroken chain is discarded rather than drawn in the wrong order. The discarded share is reported by the pipeline, and it may fall unevenly on fleets that cross several time zones.

Reproducibility

The project source code, processing scripts, visualization code, processed data, and documentation are available in the project repository.

The large raw BTS monthly files are not stored directly in GitHub. After downloading the raw files into data/raw/ , the processed visualization files can be regenerated with:

python scripts/build_data.py
python scripts/build_propagation.py

The second script builds the aircraft-rotation aggregates used by Visualizations 4 and 5, and reuses the cleaned views defined in the first so that both share one definition of an operated flight.

The website uses HTML, CSS, JavaScript, D3.js v7, TopoJSON, Python, and DuckDB.
